# Mapped & Measured: The Cost of a Claim

### The BSA Settlement Trust reports millions in administrative expenses. The harder question is what those expenses bought.

The BSA Settlement Trust exists to compensate survivors of childhood sexual abuse.

That makes its finances worth measuring in a fairly simple way: How much does the Trust spend administering claims, what does that administration produce, and how clearly can the public follow the money between the two?

The Trust's audited financial statements answer the first question.

They answer part of the second.

The third gets harder.

## $53.5 Million in One Year

The BSA Settlement Trust became effective on April 19, 2023.

During its first partial year, the Trust reported $36.1 million in operating expenses. In 2024, operating expenses increased to **$53,514,289**.

Of that 2024 total, **$36,389,886 was classified as Claims Administration**.

The Trust broke that amount into six categories:

| Claims Administration — FY2024   | Expense         |
| -------------------------------- | --------------- |
| Claims Administrator Fees        | $18,012,618     |
| Claims Management and Evaluation | $9,928,008      |
| Document Appendix                | $4,115,594      |
| Claimant Communications          | $2,560,260      |
| Independent Review Option        | $1,697,531      |
| Lien Resolution                  | $75,875         |
| **Total**                        | **$36,389,886** |

The Trust also reported approximately $2.1 million in Trustee fees and expenses, $3.6 million for Other Trust Officers and $6.35 million in legal fees.

These figures come from the Trust's audited FY2024 financial statements. BDO USA, P.C. audited those statements and reported that the supplementary schedules were reconciled to the accounting and other records underlying the financial statements.

So the money is financially accounted for at the category level.

Now measure it against output.

## What Did $53.5 Million Produce?

During 2024, the Trust reported **10,581 claim determinations**:

369 Expedited Distribution determinations, 10,194 Matrix determinations and 18 Independent Review Option determinations.

It also reported 11,095 claim disbursements totaling approximately **$55.9 million**.

That gives us several useful measurements.

Total operating expense divided by determinations comes to approximately:

**$5,058 per determination.**

Claims Administration expense divided by determinations comes to approximately:

**$3,439 per determination.**

And the two largest Claims Administration categories—Claims Administrator Fees plus Claims Management and Evaluation—totaled $27.94 million, or approximately:

**$2,641 per determination.**

Those numbers need an important qualification.

They are **not the actual cost of deciding an individual survivor's claim**.

The Trust incurred expenses for technology, communications, document management, unfinished claims, payment administration, Independent Review work and other functions. Some 2024 spending supported claims that were not completed during 2024.

These are normalized system-level measurements.

But they give us something concrete to ask about.

If the Trust spent $36.4 million administering claims during a year in which it produced 10,581 determinations, how much of that expense can be connected to the people and work that produced those determinations?

That's where the measurement becomes less clear.

## Follow the $18 Million

The largest Claims Administration line in the 2024 financial statements is:

**Claims Administrator Fees — $18,012,618.**

The financial schedule does not provide a vendor-by-vendor breakdown of that $18 million.

The second-largest category is:

**Claims Management and Evaluation — $9,928,008.**

Here the Trust provides considerably more information about function.

Its annual report explains that Claims Management and Evaluation included configuration and implementation of the claims platform and questionnaire, management of proofs of claim, evaluation of submitted documentation, payment direction, oversight, accounting and reporting.

That tells us what kinds of work were being performed.

It does not tell us how the $9.9 million was distributed among those functions.

The public financial report does not provide the personnel assigned to the work, their rates, their hours, the portion attributable to technology versus labor, or a vendor-level allocation of the expense.

That leaves a gap between financial accounting and operational accounting.

The expense can be found.

The work can be described.

Connecting one to the other is considerably harder.

## PwC Makes That Question Larger

PricewaterhouseCoopers Advisory Services LLC has since been identified in court records as performing substantial claims-administration work for the Trust.

Carrie Vos of PwC stated in a 2026 declaration that she and others at PwC had provided consulting and claims-administration services to the Settlement Trustee beginning in August 2023.

A June 2026 filing by the Future Claimants' Representative goes considerably further. It states that Trust administration costs from inception through December 31, 2025 had reached **nearly $150 million**, including approximately **$80 million in PwC fees**. The filing cites the Trust's 2023, 2024 and 2025 annual reports in support of those figures.

Those are figures asserted by the Future Claimants' Representative in litigation. They should not be confused with an independent finding that the expenditures were excessive.

But $80 million is large enough to make attribution consequential.

How many PwC personnel worked on the Trust?

At what professional levels?

At what rates?

How many hours?

How much was technology?

How much was claim evaluation?

How much was claimant support?

How much was management?

What work products correspond to those expenses?

The publicly available annual reports we have been able to examine do not provide those answers.

## Consider $2.56 Million in Communications

A smaller category makes the problem easier to see.

The Trust reported:

**Claimant Communications — $2,560,260.**

The function itself makes sense. A trust serving tens of thousands of survivors needs to communicate with claimants.

But the public financial schedule doesn't tell us what $2.56 million purchased.

How many calls were handled?

How many emails?

How many notices?

How many people performed the work?

Were they Trust personnel, PwC personnel or another vendor?

What portion was labor?

What portion was postage, telephone, software or other infrastructure?

What did one claimant interaction cost?

None of those questions establishes that $2.56 million was too much.

They establish that the category cannot be reconstructed operationally from the public financial schedule.

That distinction matters.

## What Other Abuse Trusts Disclose

The BSA Trust is unusually large. Comparing its total expenses directly with a much smaller diocesan abuse trust would be misleading.

But total dollars aren't the only thing that can be compared.

Disclosure can be compared.

The Norwich Roman Catholic Diocese Abuse Claims Trust provides a useful example.

Its governing documents identify **Craig R. Jalbert** as Trustee and establish his ordinary rate at **$540 per hour, reduced by a 10 percent public-interest discount**.

That produces an effective rate of:

**$486 per hour.**

The same structure applies a discount to professionals and paraprofessionals retained by the Trustee and provides for reporting Trustee compensation and expenses.

The Norwich claims process also identifies the person responsible for reviewing abuse claims: retired Connecticut Superior Court Judge **Salvatore C. Agati**.

Court records describe his work reviewing claims, considering additional information and conducting requested survivor interviews before determining Point Total Awards under the Trust's distribution procedures. When that work required additional time, the remaining work and deadline were addressed publicly through the court.

The significance isn't that Norwich costs less.

Its claims population is nowhere near BSA's.

The significance is that a reader can follow more of the chain:

**person → responsibility → compensation structure → work.**

## Rockville Centre Shows Something Else

The Diocese of Rockville Centre Abuse Claims Trust offers another example.

Its first annual report covered only 26 days, from the Trust's December 5, 2024 effective date through year-end. No abuse claims had yet been paid, so the report cannot responsibly be used to calculate an expense-per-claim comparison with BSA.

But the way it reports expenses is informative.

The report identifies:

**Trustee Fees — $94,572**

and then separately identifies the law firms receiving legal fees:

**Pachulski Stang Ziehl & Jones — $41,259**

**Burns Bair — $13,913**

It separately reports $38,426 in insurance expense.

Total operating expenses for the short period were $188,170.

The report also separates approximately $6.6 million of administrative liabilities assumed by the Trust at emergence from its current operating expenses. It explains that those liabilities consisted principally of pre-emergence professional expenses and U.S. Trustee fees.

Again, the dollar amounts aren't comparable to BSA.

The accounting structure is.

Instead of:

**Legal Fees — $X**

the reader gets:

**Legal Fees → named firm → amount.**

That is a meaningful difference in attribution.

## Even Enormous Bankruptcies Can Expose the Bill

There is another useful comparison outside sexual-abuse trusts.

Purdue Pharma's bankruptcy is not comparable to BSA for survivor compensation or claim valuation.

It is useful as a test of a different proposition: whether enormous scale and complexity make detailed professional-cost disclosure impractical.

Purdue's bankruptcy records include professional fee applications identifying individual professionals, billing rates, hours and categories of work.

A Davis Polk fee application covering only September through December 2025 sought approximately **$12.96 million in compensation**, accompanied by detailed billing information.

Public records in the case have exposed individual professional billing rates running into thousands of dollars per hour.

Purdue operated under a different legal fee-review regime. BSA's Settlement Trust should not be represented as legally subject to identical bankruptcy-estate billing requirements.

The comparison establishes something narrower.

**Large professional expenditures can be reported with granular attribution.**

Scale doesn't make that impossible.

## The Trustee's Compensation Shows the Difference

The BSA Trust Agreement establishes the Settlement Trustee's compensation at **$175,000 per month through December 31, 2024**, with a mechanism for subsequent adjustment.

Annualized, that is:

**$2.1 million per year.**

That should not simply be compared with Norwich's $486 hourly rate as though the jobs were identical.

They aren't necessarily identical.

But their disclosure structures are strikingly different.

At $486 an hour, a professional would need to bill approximately **360 hours in one month** to equal $175,000.

That calculation does not tell us whether $175,000 a month is reasonable.

To answer that, we would need another measurement:

**hours.**

How many hours did the BSA Settlement Trustee work?

What work did those hours produce?

How much work was performed personally by the Trustee?

How much was delegated to Claims Administrators?

How much was performed by PwC?

How much was performed by counsel and other Trust officers?

The monthly compensation amount is public.

The corresponding workload measurement is not apparent in the annual financial reports.

That prevents the most obvious reasonableness calculation from being made.

## The Missing Report

There is one more problem.

The Settlement Trust Agreement requires the Trustee to file an annual report and publish it on the Trust website when it is filed with the Bankruptcy Court. It contains a corresponding publication requirement for the Annual Claims Report.

The FY2023 report is publicly available.

The FY2024 report is publicly available.

The FY2025 Annual Report and Claims Report is confirmed by a subsequent court filing as **D.I. 215 in Case No. 20-10342**. That later filing cites D.I. 215 alongside the previous annual reports when discussing cumulative Trust administration expenses.

But D.I. 215 is not presently available on the Trust's public reports surface, and an extensive search has not located a publicly accessible Trust-hosted copy.

That does not establish that the report was never posted.

It establishes the current state:

**a report required to be published on the Trust website, and confirmed as filed with the Bankruptcy Court, is not presently available through the Trust's public reporting surface.**

That matters even more because it is the report covering the year in which cumulative administration costs reportedly approached $150 million.

## Accounted For Is Not the Same as Explained

There is no evidence in these numbers, standing alone, of fraud.

They don't establish waste.

They don't establish that PwC charged an inappropriate rate, that the Trustee was overpaid, or that any particular expense was unnecessary.

Those conclusions require evidence.

But expense categories shouldn't be asked to prove more than they actually prove either.

An audited financial statement can establish that $18 million was recorded as Claims Administrator Fees.

It doesn't necessarily tell us who received each dollar.

A financial statement can establish that $9.9 million was spent on Claims Management and Evaluation.

It doesn't necessarily tell us how many people performed that work, their rates, their hours or how the expenditure translated into completed claims.

A Trust can report $2.56 million for Claimant Communications without telling beneficiaries how many communications $2.56 million produced.

Those aren't accusations.

They're measurements of what the public record allows us to know.

For a trust established to compensate survivors of childhood sexual abuse, the relevant financial question isn't simply whether the books balance.

It is whether the money can be followed.

**Expense → recipient → personnel → rate and time → work → claim → survivor.**

The BSA Settlement Trust's public reports allow parts of that chain to be measured.

Other parts disappear inside aggregated categories.

And that leaves a substantial amount of Trust spending in an unusual position:

**financially accounted for, but not operationally explained.**

## Why 4.7%?

4.7% began with a simple question: **What happens when survivors, evidence, assets, decisions and outcomes can be seen in the same environment?**

The initiative was established to build a persistent, evidence-based record around the Boy Scouts of America settlement—one that does not reduce survivors to claims, properties to assets, or financial reports to totals without context.

We are connecting what has too often remained separated:

**Survivors → Evidence → Ground → Assets → Decisions → Money → Outcomes**

The goal is not to manufacture conclusions. It is to make the relationships between these things visible, sourced and measurable—so survivors and the public can see what happened, what can be proven, what remains unknown, and where accountability still breaks down.

Every connection strengthens the record.

**Connect to 4.7%. See what changes when the evidence is allowed to meet.**
