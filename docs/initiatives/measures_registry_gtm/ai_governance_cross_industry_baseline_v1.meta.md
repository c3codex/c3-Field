# AI Governance Cross-Industry Baseline

## Purpose
Establish a prospective baseline for industries incorporating AI at consequential scale and investing in AI governance, so later failures, cracks, remediation, and repeat patterns can be observed without hindsight fitting.

This baseline is market intelligence and longitudinal evidence. It does not redefine Measures Registry as an AI-governance product. Measures Registry remains computational systems governance: the governing object is the system and operating environment inherited by AI and other computational actors.

## Observation Sequence
`AI incorporation at scale → governance response → governance investment → operating period → observed crack/failure → consequence → remediation → additional governance investment → recurrence/non-recurrence`

For each material case preserve where evidence exists:
`baseline_date → industry → organization → AI capability → scale → inherited systems → governance control/investment → buyer → vendor → price/term → stated purpose → operating outcome → later event → consequence → remediation → additional cost → integrity mapping`

Failure classification must remain evidence-led:
- `AI_or_model_defect`
- `specialized_AI_governance_failure`
- `computational_system_or_environment_failure`
- `mixed`
- `insufficient_evidence`

Measures integrity mapping remains unchanged:
`Ownership → Authority → Position → Identity → Runtime → Evidence → Response`

`Governance Discontinuity` remains a held research candidate and may only be applied where repeated boundary reconstruction is actually evidenced.

## Top Five Baseline Markets

### Cohort 001 — Healthcare Providers
**Standing:** `active_baseline; candidate_under_validation`

**Why included:** consequential clinical and operational AI use; visible external governance procurement; formal vendor review; measurable patient, operational, legal, and financial consequences; unusually rich institutional evidence.

**Baseline signals already established:**
- healthcare-system AI governance platform purchases;
- governance/readiness consulting RFPs;
- AI governance questionnaires embedded in procurement;
- governance committees and formal policy formation;
- scaled ambient and clinical AI deployments;
- high-cost governance reconstruction before and during deployment.

**Primary observation question:** When healthcare organizations successfully or unsuccessfully govern scaled AI, which conditions belong to the AI itself and which must be reconstructed in the surrounding system/environment?

### Cohort 002 — Financial Services / Banking
**Standing:** `active_baseline; high_priority`

**Why included:** AI is moving into high-volume regulated workflows with direct financial consequence, while large institutions are scaling employee AI, customer-facing systems, fraud/risk tooling, and agentic workflows. Governance must operate across mandates, approval limits, auditability, model risk, data access, third-party systems, and runtime action.

**Current evidence signals:**
- major banks are deploying AI to very large employee populations and hundreds of use cases;
- financial institutions are moving from static-model governance toward runtime governance of agentic workflows;
- midmarket banks are expanding GenAI/agentic access while adding monitoring, accountability, and controls;
- governance questions increasingly concern complete execution trajectories, mandates, limits, approvals, and evidence rather than model output alone.

**Primary observation question:** As AI gains authority to act inside financial workflows, do failures recur at system boundaries involving mandate, approval, identity, evidence, vendor dependency, or responsibility despite mature model-risk and AI-governance controls?

### Cohort 003 — Insurance
**Standing:** `active_baseline; high_priority`

**Why included:** AI directly affects underwriting, claims, pricing, fraud, customer service, and operational decisions. Insurers face state-level examination and market-conduct scrutiny, creating unusually observable governance requirements and consequences.

**Current evidence signals:**
- 2026 industry research reports broad scaling of AI across functions while controls remain fragmented across teams and tools;
- regulators are piloting an AI Systems Evaluation Tool to examine insurer AI use, governance, risk mitigation, high-risk models, and data inputs;
- governance and compliance uncertainty remains a material barrier to further scaling.

**Primary observation question:** Where insurers have formal AI controls, do consequential failures arise from model conduct, fragmented ownership/control environments, third-party data/model relationships, evidence passage, or cross-state governance discontinuities?

### Cohort 004 — Government / Public Sector
**Standing:** `active_baseline; high_priority`

**Why included:** governments are large AI buyers; procurement makes governance requirements visible; consequential uses implicate statutory authority, public accountability, vendor responsibility, records/evidence, civil rights, and institutional ownership.

**Current evidence signals:**
- U.S. federal agencies more than doubled reported AI use from 2023 to 2024 and continued acquiring AI through FY2025;
- GAO documents multiple acquisition paths and lessons that agencies need to apply to future AI procurements;
- federal procurement policy now includes mandatory AI procurement controls, documentation standards, and contractual safeguards in some departments;
- public-sector research identifies recurring problems in evaluation criteria, vendor assessment, contract obligations, lifecycle performance, and accountability allocation.

**Primary observation question:** When a public institution procures AI, are authority, accountability, evidence, and lifecycle obligations carried coherently through the operating environment or repeatedly recreated through contract and oversight mechanisms?

### Cohort 005 — Industrial / Critical Infrastructure
**Standing:** `active_baseline; high_priority`

**Scope:** manufacturing, energy, utilities, industrial operations, robotics, physical AI, and related critical infrastructure.

**Why included:** AI is moving from advisory software into physical and operational systems where decisions can affect machinery, production, grid operations, safety, supply chains, and infrastructure. Human/AI/system relationships are increasingly real-time and cross OT/IT boundaries.

**Current evidence signals:**
- NIST's 2026 smart-manufacturing roadmap describes AI across autonomous systems, sensing, digital twins, robotics, supply chain, and industrial operations while highlighting integration, data management, reliability, explainability, and trustworthy operation;
- manufacturing governance guidance now treats governance as a practical requirement for AI deployed at scale, including autonomous procurement and factory-floor use;
- energy-industry research reports fragmented governance evidence, board-level AI-risk oversight, low readiness for independent AI-governance audit, and expanding AI-assisted control-room analytics;
- physical-AI governance work emphasizes proof of what a system decided, why, and whether unsafe outcomes were structurally prevented.

**Primary observation question:** As AI crosses from information processing into physical action, which governance failures arise from the actor and which arise from the environment's inability to carry authority, operational position, evidence, constraints, and response across IT/OT and organizational boundaries?

## Selection Rationale
These five cohorts were selected provisionally because together they maximize:
1. consequential AI deployment at meaningful scale;
2. visible or emerging governance expenditure/control formation;
3. public evidence suitable for prospective longitudinal observation;
4. materially different institutional and technical environments;
5. opportunity to test recurrence of the same system/environment cracks across industries.

The cohort set is not a claim that these are the five largest AI markets by revenue. It is a research selection optimized for Measures Registry's mission and the ability to observe governance investment and consequence.

## Cross-Industry Baseline Test
The research program will test whether increasingly sophisticated actor-centered AI governance controls successfully address AI-specific risks while a recurring class of consequential failures continues to originate in system/environment conditions such as ownership, authority, position, identity, runtime, evidence, response, and relational boundaries.

A repeated pattern across distinct industries strengthens the computational-systems-governance thesis. Non-recurrence, successful specialized governance, or evidence that failures are model-specific must be preserved equally and may weaken or narrow the thesis.

## Market / Mission Boundary
AI governance is treated as a market-intelligence and evidence surface, not as Measures Registry's product category.

Commercial research may use these cohorts to identify:
- who is spending;
- who is about to expand;
- what governance they are purchasing;
- who owns and approves the spend;
- what pressures precede purchase;
- what failures or cracks emerge afterward.

Measures Registry monetization must remain mission-consistent. No finding in this baseline authorizes product-category drift, pricing changes, outreach, publication, Registry mutation, or MAP terminology resolution.
