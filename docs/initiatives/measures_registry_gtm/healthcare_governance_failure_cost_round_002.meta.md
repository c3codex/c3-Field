# Healthcare Governance Failure and Compensatory Cost Study

## Purpose
Use healthcare as a proving ground for the necessity and economics of a coherent computational operating environment. This study does not presume healthcare is a permanent Measures Registry vertical or Registry-candidate environment.

## Governing Research Chain
`observed condition → governance failure boundary → compensating mechanism → operational/economic consequence → MR relevance`

MR relevance must be recorded conservatively as one of:
- `native_condition_candidate`
- `potentially_reducible`
- `unrelated`
- `insufficient_evidence`

No external expenditure is assumed avoidable merely because Measures Registry can describe the failure.

## Core Thesis Under Test
> Consequential computational systems operating in incoherent environments may create recurring compensatory governance costs because visibility, identity, ownership, authority, evidence, review, and lifecycle accountability must be reconstructed externally rather than carried coherently by the operating environment.

Healthcare is Case 001 because governance pressure, procurement activity, and operational consequences are unusually observable.

## F1 — Visibility / Inventory Failure
### Observed condition
AI adoption is expanding faster than provider governance structures and inventory discipline.

### Evidence
A 2025 Black Book survey summarized by Becker's Hospital Review found only 29% of hospitals had implemented and enforced AI model inventory, lineage, and sign-off policies; 48% were still drafting them. Only 22% were highly confident they could produce a complete AI audit trail within 30 days, falling to 15% among small hospitals.

Wolters Kluwer reported widespread unauthorized AI use in hospitals and health systems, including direct patient-care contexts.

### Failure boundary
The organization cannot reliably establish what AI is operating, where, for what purpose, under whose approval, or with what evidence.

### Compensating mechanisms
- AI inventory programs;
- shadow-AI discovery tools;
- governance platforms;
- additional intake/review processes;
- recurring audits and attestations.

### Consequence
Governance work shifts from native operating knowledge to reconstructive discovery and ongoing monitoring.

### Economic evidence
IBM/Ponemon's 2025 breach study found organizations with high levels of shadow AI incurred an average $670,000 higher breach cost than organizations with low or no shadow AI. This is cross-industry evidence and must not be represented as hospital-specific MR savings.

### MR relevance
`native_condition_candidate`

### Hold
Evidence supports cost associated with poor visibility; it does not yet establish how much discovery/governance spending a coherent environment could eliminate.

## F2 — Ownership / Responsibility Failure
### Observed condition
Healthcare AI responsibility is frequently distributed across IT, Quality/Safety, Compliance, clinical leadership, privacy, security, and procurement.

### Evidence
The Black Book survey found 33% of hospitals cited unclear ownership between IT, Quality/Safety, and Compliance as slowing governance progress.

Joint Commission's RUAIH certification explicitly requires a formal governance structure spanning organizational AI use rather than treating AI as solely a technology issue.

### Failure boundary
Responsibility exists institutionally but does not resolve consistently to a clear accountable actor or pathway for a specific system/use.

### Compensating mechanisms
- cross-functional governance committees;
- executive sponsorship structures;
- additional review meetings;
- escalation paths;
- role-specific policies and sign-offs.

### Consequence
Organizations add human coordination layers to reconstruct responsibility that is not evident from the operating relationship itself.

### MR relevance
`native_condition_candidate`

### Hold
Committee cost and delay have not yet been quantified sufficiently to claim avoidable governance overhead.

## F3 — Vendor Evidence / Boundary Failure
### Observed condition
Vendor-supplied AI changes over time and vendor documentation may be insufficient for hospital audit, validation, or continuing governance.

### Evidence
The Black Book survey found 41% of hospitals named limited vendor documentation such as model cards and drift reports as their top audit barrier.

The Health Sector Coordinating Council's 2026 third-party AI risk guidance calls for lifecycle monitoring, vendor-update management, revalidation, periodic reassessment, and contract-renewal review.

CommonSpirit Health publicly described rejecting an ambient-AI vendor during contract renegotiation after the vendor could not satisfy patient-data requirements; its governance committee has rejected multiple proposed use cases.

### Failure boundary
Evidence sufficient at procurement does not remain sufficient across vendor updates, model changes, new data flows, and operational use.

### Compensating mechanisms
- AI-specific vendor questionnaires;
- model cards and attestations;
- contractual disclosure/change-notification clauses;
- annual/renewal reassessment;
- technical monitoring;
- procurement and legal review.

### Consequence
Governance recurs at every vendor/change boundary because evidence and authority do not persist coherently across the lifecycle.

### MR relevance
`native_condition_candidate`

### Hold
Recurring contract-review and monitoring costs are clearly plausible but transaction-level cost evidence remains incomplete.

## F4 — Policy-to-Operation Passage Failure
### Observed condition
Hospitals may possess AI policies and committees while still lacking enforceable runtime controls, current inventories, or evidence that approved conditions persist after deployment.

### Evidence
Censinet's 2026 healthcare governance analysis distinguishes committees and policies from actual controls, noting that inventories and vendor questionnaires become stale as features, models, and data flows change.

Joint Commission RUAIH standards require ongoing monitoring, evaluation, validation, reporting, governance, and data protections throughout use—not only policy existence.

### Failure boundary
Governance is expressed in policy or committee decisions but is not carried into the operational state of the system.

### Compensating mechanisms
- governance/AI-management platforms;
- manual control testing;
- post-deployment review;
- incident reporting systems;
- additional technical monitoring and audit trails.

### Consequence
Organizations purchase or build a second technical governance layer to make policy operationally observable and enforceable.

### MR relevance
`native_condition_candidate`

### Hold
This is structurally relevant to Measures Registry but buyer evidence is still needed to show the market recognizes the distinction between policy governance and coherent operating governance.

## F5 — Procurement / Review Duplication
### Observed condition
AI is creating additional procurement screening, risk-tiering, vendor-review, contract, and renewal processes on top of existing security and technology review.

### Evidence
JPS Health Network has incorporated a dedicated AI Governance Questionnaire into procurement review.

Health-sector guidance recommends risk-based AI review across procurement, deployment, vendor change, monitoring, renewal, and retirement.

### Failure boundary
Existing procurement records do not contain enough persistent, system-specific evidence about AI use, authority, risk, data handling, model changes, and review conditions.

### Compensating mechanisms
- AI intake forms;
- AI-specific questionnaires;
- separate governance committee review;
- legal clauses and attestations;
- repeated review at renewal or material change.

### Consequence
The same system relationship must be re-described and re-evaluated across multiple organizational boundaries.

### MR relevance
`potentially_reducible`

### Hold
Need transaction/workflow evidence showing duplicated review and measurable time/cost before asserting economic reduction.

## F6 — Governance Failure Cost Evidence
### Supported cost signals
1. IBM/Ponemon: high shadow-AI use associated with approximately $670K higher average breach cost than low/no shadow AI across surveyed organizations.
2. Healthcare breach costs remain among the highest by industry; this establishes consequence magnitude but not an MR-attributable savings estimate.
3. Provider organizations are purchasing recurring platforms, consulting, monitoring, contract review, and governance structures, demonstrating ongoing compensatory spend.

### Unsupported claims — HOLD
Do not claim:
- MR would eliminate healthcare governance spending;
- a particular governance platform/consulting contract is unnecessary;
- the $670K differential is healthcare-specific;
- a coherent environment would prevent all AI incidents;
- a specific contract value is recoverable savings.

## Proving-Ground Reframe
Healthcare standing for this research program:
`proving_ground_candidate`

Healthcare is used to test whether coherent operating-environment failures have observable governance and economic consequences. It is not presumed to be the permanent Measures Registry market, product boundary, or Registry-participation model.

## New Primary Research Questions
1. Where does governance fail first as AI crosses organizational/system boundaries?
2. Which compensating controls are added because the operating environment cannot natively preserve visibility, authority, responsibility, evidence, and review?
3. Which compensating controls recur over time or across contracts?
4. What are the measurable labor, delay, contract, platform, legal, monitoring, remediation, and renewal costs?
5. Which costs are intrinsic to responsible governance and which are reconstructive/duplicative?
6. Can the same failure classes be observed outside healthcare?
7. Does a coherent operating environment measurably reduce the need for reconstructive governance without weakening legitimate oversight?

## Next Evidence Work
### A. Follow three named provider cases end-to-end
For each, capture:
`system/use → failure boundary → governance response → vendor/contract/process added → recurring obligation → measurable cost/delay → unresolved condition`

Priority candidates:
- Hawaii Health Systems Corporation;
- Mercyhealth;
- Tahoe Forest Health System.

### B. Quantify compensatory governance
Search for:
- contract terms and renewal periods;
- governance-platform licensing/implementation;
- consulting retainers;
- staffing/FTE additions;
- committee cadence and review burden;
- procurement cycle length;
- legal/vendor-review requirements;
- reassessment and monitoring frequency;
- cost of remediation or failed pilots.

### C. Build the failure taxonomy before the sales message
Do not tailor the Healthcare AI Environment Assessment around healthcare compliance categories yet. First determine whether the same failure boundaries reliably recur and whether they map to Measures Registry's native system/environment relationships.
