# Physical Substrate, Capital Concentration, and Externalized Burden — Round 001

## Research question

As AI increases computational intensity around largely unchanged business activity, what physical resources, capital structures, supply-chain dependencies, and external burdens are required to sustain the resulting compute buildout?

## Evidence summary

### 1. AI compute is becoming an industrial-scale physical load

IEA reports global data-centre electricity use at roughly 415 TWh in 2024 and projects around 945 TWh by 2030. In the United States, data centres are projected to account for nearly half of electricity-demand growth to 2030. Advanced AI server power density rose roughly 11x from 2020 to 2025 and is projected to rise further by 2027.

Interpretation: the marginal computational layer is becoming physically legible in grid, generation, transformer, switchgear, cooling, land, and water demand.

### 2. Critical-mineral concentration creates sovereign dependency

IEA estimates data-centre gallium demand in 2030 could exceed 10% of today's supply. China accounts for approximately 95-99% of refined/primary low-purity gallium supply depending on source and definition. USGS documents recent Chinese gallium export controls, including the December 2024 U.S. ban and the November 2025 temporary lifting.

IEA's 2026 critical-minerals outlook also finds high concentration in refining/processing across multiple minerals; China accounted for about half of global copper-smelting capacity by 2025 and dominates rare-earth processing.

Interpretation: a data centre may be financed, built, and governed domestically while inheriting material choke points controlled outside its authority domain.

### 3. Capital is converging around compute infrastructure

Reuters reported Nvidia partnering with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR on platforms intended to raise more than $500B of third-party capital for AI infrastructure. Broadcom/Apollo/Blackstone separately formed an AI XPV platform with an initial $35B transaction and a target exceeding 20 GW of compute capacity through 2028. BlackRock-managed funds hold 80% of the roughly $14B Meta El Paso data-centre venture.

Interpretation: AI infrastructure is increasingly financed as a long-duration heavy-asset class rather than merely as technology capex.

### 4. The same capital logic extends into mining

BlackRock publicly argued in 2026 for greater mining-sector scale and consolidation because AI, electrification, and defence are accelerating commodity demand while supply investment remains inadequate. BlackRock is a stakeholder in BHP, Rio Tinto, and Glencore. BHP reported copper as its largest earnings contributor in FY2026 and linked future copper growth partly to AI infrastructure demand.

This does not establish coordinated control across mining and AI infrastructure. It does establish that major asset managers explicitly recognize AI infrastructure as a driver of mining-capital allocation and may hold exposure across both layers.

### 5. Power-equipment bottlenecks are already operational

Reuters reported generator step-up transformer lead times exceeding 160 weeks in early 2026 and high-voltage circuit-breaker lead times around 125 weeks, with data-centre construction becoming a material share of electrical-equipment demand.

Interpretation: the compute build is constrained not only by chips but by slow-cycle industrial assets and manufacturing capacity.

### 6. AI infrastructure is creating new energy-resource relationships

Examples include large co-located data-centre/power projects such as the proposed Brookfield/NextEra Paducah, Kentucky development, combining data centres, natural gas generation, and battery storage on a former uranium-enrichment site. Uranium investment interest is also rising as nuclear is repositioned as a potential source of firm power for data-centre demand.

Interpretation: compute infrastructure is becoming entangled with generation, fuel, storage, and sovereign energy policy.

### 7. The burdens do not fall only on investors or AI buyers

Reuters documents growing opposition to data centres over electricity prices, water use, pollution, land use, and grid reliability. New York imposed a one-year moratorium on large new data centres in July 2026; Pennsylvania imposed stricter environmental/transparency/community-approval requirements in August 2026. A Reuters/Ipsos poll found only 33% supported the current rapid pace of U.S. data-centre construction and only 14% supported a nearby project.

Reuters also documented pollution concerns around xAI's Tennessee/Mississippi power build, where 59 gas turbines were reportedly installed without federal Clean Air Act permits and nearby communities bore potential air-quality impacts.

Separately, AP reported rapid foreign-backed rare-earth exploration in Brazil, including projects near protected and Indigenous lands, with concerns over deforestation, water pollution, toxic/radioactive waste, and resource sovereignty.

Interpretation: the AI-compute stack externalizes part of its physical burden onto communities, grids, ratepayers, water systems, mining regions, and jurisdictions that may receive a much smaller share of the financial upside.

## Emerging chain

unchanged business activity
→ higher computational intensity
→ larger inference/agentic workload
→ chips/network/storage
→ data centres
→ grid equipment and generation
→ minerals/refining/fuels
→ concentrated supply dependencies
→ financing and asset ownership
→ local environmental/social/grid burdens

## Governance significance

The infrastructure build creates consequential relationships that cross multiple independent authority domains:

- enterprise ↔ model/cloud provider
- data-centre operator ↔ utility/grid operator
- utility ↔ equipment supplier
- equipment/chip producer ↔ mineral/refining supply chain
- developer ↔ local jurisdiction/community
- financier ↔ infrastructure owner/tenant
- state ↔ strategic mineral supplier

The deeper systems-governance question is therefore not only whether AI is governed. It is whether the full physical and institutional relationship chain remains visible, bounded, current, and accountable as computational demand propagates through domains that do not share one authority.

## Strongest current finding

> The AI infrastructure boom is converting increased computational intensity into a cross-domain physical dependency chain whose financial returns and physical burdens are distributed unevenly across actors and jurisdictions.

## Falsifiers / holds

- Do not infer coordinated ownership or control merely because the same asset manager holds positions across infrastructure and mining.
- Do not attribute all critical-mineral demand growth to AI; electrification, defence, vehicles, grids, and other sectors are major drivers.
- Copper forecasts remain uncertain and architecture/efficiency changes may reduce copper intensity.
- Many announced gigawatt projects may not be built on schedule; financing and grid constraints remain material.
- Public opposition and environmental burden vary sharply by location and project design.

## Next research target

Trace where value and governance authority diverge across the chain: who owns/finances each layer, who has operational authority, who bears resource/environmental risk, and where dependency becomes a strategic or governance choke point.
