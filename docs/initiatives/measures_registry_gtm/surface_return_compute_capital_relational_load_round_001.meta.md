# Surface Return — Compute, Capital, and Relational Load

## Research question

If large institutions already modernized their systems before the present AI boom and their underlying businesses have not fundamentally changed, why is AI driving another extraordinary wave of compute and infrastructure spending?

## Surface finding

The evidence supports a distinction between business-output continuity and computational-intensity expansion.

The underlying businesses remain recognizably the same, but AI adoption inserts additional computational operations, model calls, retrieval, orchestration, monitoring, evaluation, and agentic loops into existing workflows. This changes the computational path through the business more than the business itself.

The resulting capital chain is increasingly:

power -> data center -> networking -> accelerator/chip -> model -> cloud/model access -> orchestration/agents -> enterprise workflow -> monitoring/governance -> business outcome

## Capital concentration

2026 infrastructure spending remains extreme. Public estimates and company guidance place annual hyperscaler capex in the hundreds of billions of dollars per firm or group, with the four largest operators collectively on track for well above $600B and revised estimates above $700B in 2026. The current market is also using debt, leases, special-purpose vehicles, long-term energy agreements, and infrastructure partnerships to extend the build beyond balance-sheet capex.

This supports a capital-system interpretation: compute is being financed as a durable industrial input rather than as a one-time software purchase.

## Downstream value gap

Enterprise evidence does not show proportionate value realization yet.

KPMG Q2 2026 reports only 26% of organizations with real-time visibility into AI operating cost, only 7% with established ROI in its global pulse, and average planned US AI investment around $202M over the next 12 months. EY reports 82% of senior leaders investing in AI are concerned about token usage and related cost. PwC reports only about one in eight CEOs seeing both higher revenue and lower costs from AI.

This means capital formation upstream is moving faster than cost visibility and proven economic return downstream.

## Relational-load hypothesis

The same business relationship can now generate many more computational relationships.

Example:

customer <-> institution
institution <-> model provider
agent <-> internal application
agent <-> identity service
model <-> enterprise data
agent <-> vendor API
reviewer <-> agent output
monitor <-> runtime
policy engine <-> action
auditor <-> retained evidence

The business transaction may be unchanged while the number of consequential computational relationships expands materially.

Working proposition:

> AI increases computational intensity and relational load around existing economic activity faster than it changes the underlying business function.

This gives a direct bridge from infrastructure capital to computational-systems governance.

## Governance implication

If an institution modernized its own internal environment before AI, large-scale AI deployment can still reconnect that coherent environment to external infrastructure, models, vendors, agents, and services outside the institution's authority domain.

The prior modernization therefore does not eliminate the governance problem. It can expose a new one:

> internally coherent institutions become dependent on increasingly external, shared, changing, compute-intensive systems whose consequential relationships cross authority boundaries.

The governance burden scales not simply with the number of models or employees, but with the number and volatility of consequential relationships through which authority and effect pass.

## Economic test

The critical economic question is no longer only whether AI increases productivity.

It is:

> Does the additional computational intensity create enough measurable value to justify the infrastructure, integration, monitoring, governance, and reconstruction costs required to keep the resulting system coherent?

A second test follows:

> Where does value accumulate when computational intensity increases around an unchanged business function?

Candidate capture layers:
- chips/accelerators
- power generation and grid capacity
- data centers and cooling
- networking
- cloud infrastructure
- model providers
- orchestration and agent platforms
- observability/security/governance tooling
- financing and infrastructure funds

Enterprise value capture remains comparatively uneven and less visible.

## Falsifiers

This interpretation weakens if:
1. enterprise operating income/revenue growth attributable to AI begins matching or exceeding the growth in compute and infrastructure cost;
2. agentic workflows materially reduce total computational and governance cost per business outcome rather than merely adding steps;
3. infrastructure overbuild is absorbed quickly without continued dependence on high utilization, subsidies, debt, or speculative future demand;
4. cross-domain AI deployment does not materially increase governance/reconstruction burden.

## Research disposition

The data-center build should not be treated as separate from the governance thesis.

It appears to be the physical manifestation of a larger transition:

> computation is becoming a persistent metered operating input across existing institutions, and the number of consequential computational relationships is rising faster than the underlying number of business relationships.

If that pattern holds, the economic relevance of computational-systems governance comes from governing the relational load created by this new computational layer rather than governing AI as an isolated actor.
