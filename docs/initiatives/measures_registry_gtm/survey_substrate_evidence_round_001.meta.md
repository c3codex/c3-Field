## Research question
Do recent surveys and disclosure studies show that organizations actually possess and operationalize the governance artifacts needed to keep consequential AI/agent activity visible, current, attributable, and governable across change?

## Evidence standard
This round prioritizes direct survey/report sources and preserves sponsor/conflict notes. Vendor-commissioned surveys are treated as directional evidence, not neutral population estimates.

## Findings

### 1. Governance maturity remains uncommon at agentic scale
Deloitte's 2026 State of AI research surveyed 3,235 IT and business leaders across 24 countries. Only 21% reported a mature governance model for agentic AI, while close to three-quarters planned agentic deployment within two years.

Interpretation: deployment ambition materially exceeds mature governance capacity.

Source: Deloitte, 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise / agentic governance summary.

### 2. Re-review after system change is weak
EDUCAUSE's 2025 QuickPoll found that among institutions with AI-related procurement processes, only 36% said AI products were subsequently reviewed against AI-related procurement questions to account for new functions and developments. The same study identified rapid product change (45%) and insufficient institutional AI governance (40%) as the two leading procurement challenges.

Interpretation: artifact existence at procurement does not imply artifact continuity after change.

Source quality: sector association survey; direct higher-education population evidence.

### 3. Existing policy often does not bind the live environment
EDUCAUSE reported only 9% of respondents believed existing cybersecurity/privacy policies sufficiently addressed AI-related risks; 42% rated them only somewhat adequate. Institutions were actively modifying data governance, privacy, and cyber policies as AI entered existing systems.

Interpretation: inherited governance artifacts often require reconstruction or extension when computational capability changes.

### 4. Incident-response artifacts are missing despite live agent access
Grant Thornton's 2026 AI Impact Survey reports that nearly three in four organizations are giving agentic AI access to data and processes, while only 20% have a tested AI incident response plan.

Interpretation: runtime exposure can precede verified response/disposition capability.

Conflict note: consulting-firm survey; commercially interested source. Use as directional evidence pending independent corroboration.

### 5. Access-control and shadow-AI gaps are visible in breach data
IBM/Ponemon's 2025 Cost of a Data Breach research, based on 600 breached organizations globally, found 63% either lacked an AI governance policy or were still developing one. Of those with governance policies, only 34% regularly audited for unsanctioned AI. Among organizations reporting AI-related breaches, 97% lacked proper AI access controls. High-shadow-AI organizations averaged $670,000 higher breach costs than low/no-shadow-AI organizations.

Interpretation: policy artifact presence is distinct from active inventory, access control, and audit correspondence.

Conflict note: Ponemon-conducted, IBM-sponsored/analyzed study. Stronger than a marketing poll because tied to observed breach cases, but sponsor interest remains relevant.

### 6. Agent inventory and lifecycle custody may be materially weak
Cloud Security Alliance's 2026 survey reported 82% of organizations had unknown AI agents in their infrastructure and only 21% had formal agent decommissioning processes. Sixty-five percent reported an agent-related incident in the prior year.

Interpretation: identity/inventory and lifecycle-disposition artifacts may fail to remain complete as agent populations proliferate.

Conflict note: CSA report commissioned by Token Security, an identity-security vendor. Treat absolute percentages cautiously; retain as directional signal.

### 7. Identity governance is still inherited from unsuitable human/service-account models
Okta's 2026 Global CISO Insights reports 25% of CISOs treat agents the same as human identities, 21% use shared credentials/service accounts with broad permissions, and 20% leave agent management ad hoc to deploying teams.

Interpretation: identity artifacts frequently exist, but may not represent the actual authority/lifecycle properties of autonomous agents.

Conflict note: identity-platform vendor survey; use for pattern support, not population certainty.

### 8. Observability is a named operationalization gap
PwC's 2025 US Responsible AI Survey found half of respondents identified translating principles into scaled operational processes as the largest hurdle. PwC describes mature programs as moving toward continuous monitoring, observability, inventory, testing, and feedback loops rather than static policy governance.

Interpretation: market maturity itself is being defined as transition from document/policy artifact to live system correspondence.

Conflict note: consulting-firm survey with commercial incentive; useful because framing is consistent with other independent/sector findings.

### 9. Public-company board oversight is rising faster than operational disclosure
EY's 2025 review of Fortune 100 disclosures found 48% disclosed AI in board risk oversight, up from 16% in 2024; 40% disclosed at least one board committee overseeing AI. But only 16% disclosed management reporting structure to the board and only 9% disclosed reporting frequency.

Interpretation: formal oversight artifacts are proliferating, while evidence of continuous operational reporting remains much less visible.

Source quality: disclosure analysis, not respondent self-report. Absence of disclosure does not prove absence of practice.

## Cross-source substrate pattern
Across education, enterprise, breach, identity, and board-governance evidence, the recurring weak points are:

1. inventory completeness;
2. ownership/accountability;
3. identity and permission representation;
4. change-triggered re-review;
5. continuous monitoring / runtime correspondence;
6. incident response and disposition;
7. lifecycle/decommissioning;
8. portability of governance state beyond initial approval.

This supports a narrower proposition than "organizations lack AI governance":

> Governance artifacts may exist while failing to remain complete, current, runtime-bound, or operationally usable as computational actors, permissions, vendors, and capabilities change.

## Falsification condition
The Measures Registry differentiation weakens if higher-quality longitudinal evidence shows that most institutions already maintain complete, current, verifiable, portable governance artifacts across identity, authority, inventory, monitoring, change, response, and lifecycle at low recurring cost.

## Current disposition
status: supported_directionally_not_yet_population_proven

The substrate problem is visible in multiple independent evidence classes, but prevalence estimates vary by survey design and sponsor. Continue prioritizing regulator, audit, sector-association, disclosure-analysis, and observed-incident evidence over vendor-sponsored percentages.
